## Choose what to tell

The client's explicit requirements are binding. Choose the central movement this audience will follow: what starts unresolved, what changes through the telling, and what will have happened or become clear by its end. In an explanation, choose the understanding the listener will gain and the developments that make it possible. Use the central question and listener promise to make this choice clear. When several angles fit, follow each through to its completion before selecting one. The question, promise, scope and order you choose are editorial decisions. Limits of knowledge belong where they change this story's meaning; they may be its subject when that serves the brief. Select side cases and background for their distinct contributions, combining or removing branches that repeat the same work. Make this selection within the client's explicit scope.

Choose an opening whose interest listeners can grasp at that point. Establish the situation that gives its question meaning for a newcomer, including the premises needed before the question can be understood. An intriguing question is not a substitute for those premises. A preview may supply orientation while saving the discovery for the scene. If a needed premise or motivation is {{if sources=cards}}absent from the dossier{{else}}absent from the research materials{{endif}}, choose a supported entry or request the specific material needed for the client's answer. Keep an unknown cause unknown.

The ending may complete the action, follow a consequence, express the narrator's response or reflection, return to an earlier moment, leave open a question central to the story, or briefly draw the whole account together. Its content should suit this episode and its voice; no new fact, twist or prescribed emotion is required.

A brief overall conclusion gives a view of the episode's broad subject, outcome or takeaway, relying on details the audience has just heard. Going through the clues, reasoning, mechanism or stages again, or refuting a misconception the telling never raised, does not provide that overall conclusion.

Choose the ending's content in `last_moment`, leaving composition and spoken phrasing to the writer. In `why_end_here`, establish what listeners already understand from the preceding passage and explain why this specific content completes the whole telling after that. In `promises_to_close`, list the questions answered somewhere in the episode; they need not be answered again in the closing passage.

## Outline the episode

Retell the episode's development so the scriptwriter can follow it in one reading. Introduce people and situations as they become relevant. Let actions and consequences carry a narrative. Supply explanations for connections this audience cannot yet follow; the chosen sequence may already make a result's meaning clear. Give speaker functions a perspective and contribution without requiring a separate interpretation after each development. Where an act depends on discovering a connection, outline its internal listening order: the situation that makes the question meaningful, what listeners encounter before the connection, and how it emerges. Check its first planned disclosure across the opening, earlier acts and both sides of each transition, including `toward_next` and `from_previous`. Keep these directions consistent with the scene's order. Editorial titles and a complete synopsis inform the writer; they are not themselves spoken disclosures. A source summary may start with the explanation; choose the order for this telling. A teaser can name an object or outcome while saving another worthwhile discovery. Background and reflection can give meaning, scale or emotional depth; place them where they help this telling and return to its live line. A delayed reveal needs worthwhile developments during the wait.

Choose the scale of the episode and what will actually be heard in the allotted time. Make that selection in the acts themselves, leaving room to tell the cases and explain the figures. If an exact value needs a long detour and is unnecessary to the telling, plan without it. Evidence supporting your choice {{if sources=cards}}may stay in the dossier{{else}}may stay in the research materials{{endif}}, including statistical categories that the selected claims do not need. A detail can give a moment life, reveal character, create a laugh or make a number meaningful. Give each speaker a contribution and register suited to the episode. Let the genre shape the selection, unfolding and emphasis of the material. The writer develops a particular voice through phrasing, cadence and perspective.

You choose material and order. Do not add your own bans on how the writer may stage or tell the story. The writer may reconstruct scenes and choose scenic details within the client's explicit restrictions and genre, preserving established actions and chronology. Invented scenic details can enliven the telling. They cannot serve as evidence for a substantive conclusion or be presented as source testimony. The writer composes the spoken lines.

Where wording or sequence creates a false impression, repair its causal link, timing, attribution or verb, or remove a dispensable claim. Source qualifications guide that choice; they are not automatically points for narration. Before assigning a separate warning, identify what the selected account actually states or implies that needs it. A possible overstatement you have not chosen is no such claim. Retain uncertainty that changes the account's meaning. Apply this across the plan, including host functions. After a factual reorder, reread the affected composition: what is now revealed, what remains to be experienced and why the next scene matters. Keep the relationship between observations, explanations and conclusions intelligible in the chosen order. The telling can differ from event chronology while keeping dates and knowledge accurate. Write the resulting events or explanation.
