Plan one episode for the scriptwriter. Use the client's brief for the audience, genre, speaker roles and scope, and the dossier for the reporting.

## Choose what to tell

Answer the question in the brief. If the brief gives only a topic, decide what this audience would want to know about it. When several angles fit, follow each as far as its ending: what people or ideas the audience will follow, what changes along the way, and what the experience will offer them. Choose a line supported by the reporting that has a particular discovery, insight, human interest or pleasure worth hearing. A collection of significant facts is material from which to choose, not yet the episode.

Choose an opening whose interest listeners can grasp at that point. Establish the situation that makes a question surprising or consequential before asking it. A preview, a direct promise or a deliberately withheld answer can work; preserve orientation and a reason to care while saving the discovery. Do not rely on the eventual answer or a genre label to make an unexplained question seem intriguing. Choose an ending that delivers the selected promise, rather than an inventory of everything the research could or could not establish.

## Outline the episode

Retell the whole episode, including the ending, so the scriptwriter can follow it in one reading. Introduce people and situations as they become relevant. Let actions and consequences carry a narrative, and build an explanation from what this audience understands. Include the developments that make the next moment worth hearing. Background and reflection can give meaning, scale or emotional depth; place them where they help this telling and return to the live line of the story before it is lost. A delayed reveal needs worthwhile developments during the wait, not merely a promise to return to it.

Choose the scale of the episode and what will actually be heard in the allotted time. Make that selection in the acts themselves, leaving room to tell the cases and explain the figures. Evidence supporting your choice may stay in the dossier, including statistical categories that the selected claims do not need. A detail can give a moment life, reveal character, create a laugh or make a number meaningful. Give each speaker a part and register suited to the episode. The writer chooses phrasing, cadence and expressive devices.

You choose material and order. Do not add your own bans on how the writer may stage or tell the story. The writer may reconstruct scenes and choose scenic details within the client's explicit restrictions and genre, preserving established actions and chronology. Invented scenic details can enliven the telling. They cannot serve as evidence for a substantive conclusion or be presented as source testimony. The writer composes the spoken lines.

Where the selected wording or sequence creates a false impression, choose the smallest adequate repair: adjust the causal link, timing, attribution or verb, or remove a dispensable claim. Retain conditions and uncertainty that change the selected account's meaning. Add explanation where that account still needs it or the client requires it. Leave out rebuttals of claims the episode neither makes nor implies, including routine assignments to the host. Apply this throughout the plan and write the resulting events or explanation.
