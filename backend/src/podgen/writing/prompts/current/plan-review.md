Distinguish client requirements from the planner's working question, promise and order. Judge those choices by the story they make possible. A plan's addition does not become a client obligation. If the opening needs a premise the material does not supply, request another supported entry or the specific missing research rather than an invented motivation.

For the opening, use what listeners can know at that point, not the answer you learn later in the plan. Is the situation clear enough for a newcomer to understand the question, why it matters and why these events belong together? Distinguish new subject knowledge from ordinary inferences listeners can draw from introduced facts. Identify the missing fact or connection that prevents them from following the next development; once that gap is filled, the account can move on. Identify any premise the plan assumes before it has supplied it; an attractive question alone does not prepare that premise. Check the proposed listening order inside discovery-led acts as well as between them. Locate the first planned disclosure across the opening, earlier acts and both transition directions, including `toward_next` and `from_previous`. Editorial titles and a complete synopsis are writer knowledge, not automatically speech. Judge the listening order they prescribe. If an earlier handoff gives away a scene's connection, repairing that scene alone cannot restore its discovery. Identify what is spent and what the remaining passage offers; request a coherent order or selection. A teaser may name an object or outcome while saving something else worth hearing; do not require every answer to be hidden. Merely finding a matching payoff later does not establish that the opening works. Through the middle, assess whether discoveries and changing expectations sustain a delay or whether a detour leaves it behind.

The planner chooses the episode's scale and the material that will be heard. Check that a central story has actually been selected. A side branch needs a distinct contribution; several branches that establish the same thing can require combination or removal rather than another transition or more explanation. Assess that selection, including numbers and vivid details, by what it contributes within the allotted time. When supplied, use `duration_context`, the writer's rough text-length estimate, to assess the scale and room for development; it is not measured audio or an exact word quota. If an unnecessary exact value needs a long detour, request an account without it. Supporting evidence and statistical categories need not all become speech. The acts should select the version to be heard: which events or explanation to develop, which details listeners need, and what they learn. An optional-material list does not make this choice for the act. Speaker functions should support the selected telling.

Assess whether that selection leaves time to tell and experience the material. If an act is overloaded, require a choice in the act itself. Check that the act's chosen detail and the optional extensions or cuts agree; a selection stated only in timing assumptions will not tell the writer what to develop. Editorial choices should name the material itself, with catalog locators kept in reference fields. Background, reflection, humor and sensory detail may give meaning or pleasure without a new fact. After a chronology or attribution repair, reassess the composition: has a result arrived before the scenes that made it interesting, or has their discovery already been spent in the preview? Check the chronology of actions, causes and what each person could know at the time, including implications created by transitions. A nonchronological telling must not imply that a later discovery caused an earlier decision. Accurate event dates do not dictate the order of telling. Request restructuring when a factual patch has weakened the story.

Distinguish a planner's selection from an imposed restriction on the writer. The writer may reconstruct scenes and choose scenic details within the client's explicit restrictions and genre, preserving established actions and chronology. Invented scenic details can enliven the telling. They cannot serve as evidence for a substantive conclusion or be presented as source testimony. Request removal of a planner's unauthorized restriction while preserving the selected story.

For a required factual finding, name the actual misleading claim or implication and the words or sequence that create it. Request the smallest adequate repair, which may be a change in wording, timing or attribution, or deletion of a dispensable claim. Request an added fact or statistical category only when a retained thought needs it for accuracy or understanding, or the client explicitly requires it. Explain that dependency. Preserve meaningful conditions and uncertainty, and check the account across all fields. A later or secondhand report does not by itself contradict an earlier account. Distinguish missing confirmation in the supplied excerpts from evidence of an error.

Require revision for a misleading account, an unprepared opening question, a substantially overloaded or stalled route, a restriction that improperly binds the writer, or instructions that take the place of the story. A rebuttal of a proposition the account neither states nor implies requires an `important` finding and `revise` when assigned as a serious precaution or included as one in the telling, unless it serves an explicit client request or a clarification essential to understanding. Judge an intentional joke, irony or reflective aside by its contribution in context, without treating every mention of an alternative as a warning. Accept a usable plan with no required repairs or blocking evidence gaps. Optional suggestions may improve an accepted plan. Use `needs_research` only for a specific evidence gap preventing an adequate version. Repairs possible with existing material belong under `revise`.
