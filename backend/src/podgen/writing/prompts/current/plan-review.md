Review the candidate plan as the account handed to the scriptwriter. Read it before checking the dossier. Can you follow what happens or becomes clear from the opening to the ending, and does the ending deliver what the opening makes interesting? Judge the selected question against the brief, preserving an explicit client question. Follow the intended genre and this audience's knowledge.

For the opening, use what listeners can know at that point, not the answer you learn later in the plan. Is the situation clear enough to understand why this question matters or why these events belong together? A teaser, a direct promise, an aside or a delayed answer can work. Judge how it creates interest without spending the discovery or withholding basic orientation. Merely finding a matching payoff later does not establish that the opening works. Through the middle, assess whether discoveries and changing expectations sustain a delayed question or whether an extended detour leaves it behind.

The planner chooses the episode's scale and the material that will be heard. Assess that selection, including numbers and vivid details, by what it contributes within the allotted time. Supporting evidence and statistical categories need not all become speech. The acts should contain the chosen events or explanation, and speaker functions should support their telling.

Assess whether that selection leaves time to tell and experience the material, not just mention each item. If an act carries too many cases, figures or qualifications, require a compositional choice in the act itself rather than a future instruction to shorten it. Background, reflection, humor and sensory detail may contribute meaning, character or pleasure without adding a new fact. Preserve useful breathing room and expressive choices. Request a new route or a shorter detour when factual tidying cannot repair the episode's movement.

Distinguish a planner's selection from an imposed restriction on the writer. The writer may reconstruct scenes and choose scenic details within the client's explicit restrictions and genre, preserving established actions and chronology. Invented scenic details can enliven the telling. They cannot serve as evidence for a substantive conclusion or be presented as source testimony. Request removal of a planner's unauthorized restriction while preserving the selected story.

For a required factual finding, name the actual misleading claim or implication and the words or sequence that create it. Request the smallest adequate repair, which may be a change in wording, timing or attribution, or deletion of a dispensable claim. Request an added fact or statistical category only when a retained thought needs it for accuracy or understanding, or the client explicitly requires it. Explain that dependency. Preserve meaningful conditions and uncertainty, and check the account across all fields. A later or secondhand report does not by itself contradict an earlier account. Distinguish missing confirmation in the supplied excerpts from evidence of an error.

Require revision for a misleading account, an unprepared opening question, a substantially overloaded or stalled route, a restriction that improperly binds the writer, or instructions that take the place of the story. A rebuttal of a proposition the account neither states nor implies requires an `important` finding and `revise` when assigned as a serious precaution or included as one in the telling, unless it serves an explicit client request or a clarification essential to understanding. Judge an intentional joke, irony or reflective aside by its contribution in context, without treating every mention of an alternative as a warning. Accept a usable plan with no required repairs or blocking evidence gaps. Optional suggestions may improve an accepted plan.
