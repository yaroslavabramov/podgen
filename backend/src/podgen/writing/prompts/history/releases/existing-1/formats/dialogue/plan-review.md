## Follow the story

Reconstruct the opening using only what the listener knows by then. Can they grasp the situation before the question depends on it? For each act, identify what someone does, observes or discovers, or what becomes intelligible through an example or comparison. Then ask what changes because of it. If the only answer is an instruction to explain, qualify or warn, locate the missing development in the available material. Request a change to that act and the transitions that depend on it.

Compare each successive case with what the earlier scenes have established. What new possibility, consequence, perspective or pleasure does it bring? If removing or combining a case would lose nothing the telling needs, identify the stronger passage to develop. Detail, a joke or a return to an earlier scene can earn its time through its effect on a listener. For a plan with `story_selection`, assess the variant developed in the acts. The other concept helps explain the choice and carries no obligation for the writer.

Follow each important discovery through the opening, acts and both sides of the transitions, including `toward_next` and `from_previous`. Distinguish knowledge given to the writer in a title or synopsis from a planned spoken disclosure. If a transition gives away the next scene's entire payoff, locate both passages. If an opening withholds its payoff, check that the intervening developments sustain interest. Ask which new fact the audience needs and which ordinary inferences listeners can draw for themselves.

Compare the episode-wide answer with the last moment. Read `why_end_here`: what completes this particular story there? A discovery, a reflection or a limit of knowledge can earn that place. An accurate synopsis repeated after the last event can undo an ending. If the ending is missing, ask for a completion supported by the reporting, without imposing a particular moral or emotional effect.

## Check what the material supports

For a factual finding identify the exact claim or implication and its consequence. Check dates, attribution, causality and what people could know at each decision. A later or secondhand source can coexist with earlier knowledge. A source limit can call for a narrower verb, attribution or a cut. When a plan assigns a warning or a detour to establish uncertainty, read the affected sequence with just the precise claim in place. If that suffices, request the change in the act itself and keep its action moving, especially on the approach to the ending. Keep a dispute or caveat that genuinely changes events or their meaning. Judge irony by its effect in the passage. A serious rebuttal to a claim the account never makes requires revision when the planner assigns it to the writer.

Check the detail chosen in the acts against the allotted time and `duration_context`, when supplied. If a detour crowds out the scene, name what to condense or remove. A list of optional cuts cannot fix an overfull act on its own. Require an additional fact or category only when the client's request or the retained account depends on it. Preserve useful material and the writer's freedom to stage and phrase it within the brief. Reject invented evidence and unauthorized restrictions on scenic invention.
