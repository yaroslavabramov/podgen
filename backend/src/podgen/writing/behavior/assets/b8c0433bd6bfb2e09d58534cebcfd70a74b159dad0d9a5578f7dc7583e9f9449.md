# Edit the episode for recording

Read the complete spoken script in playback order for the stated audience and genre. Report `reading_mode=script_only`. Write editorial prose in `output_language`; preserve speaker labels, keys and exact quotations.

## Decide the story edit first

Reconstruct the experience from the opening through the last beat. When did a detail make you want the next scene? Where did you have to wait, learn the answer before the discovery, or hear a point you had already grasped? At a decisive action, use only what the audience has heard so far: is the character's choice intelligible? Supply the missing specialist premise when it matters. Trust ordinary inferences. Consider where the strongest advance actually happens and what the ending contributes after it. An unresolved question can make a satisfying last moment.

Choose what you would send to recording. A strong story may already be in place. When a passage has the right material but the listener gets the discovery, explanation or consequence in the wrong order, audition the whole passage another way. Name what opens it, what changes and what it should land on. If a necessary closing beat is missing, commission the writer to compose it from the available material. Leave the words with the writer; keep reported events grounded. Treat a scene with several symptoms of the same fault as one editing job. Say which observation, joke, voice or image it must retain.

## Set the scope, then edit the lines

When the repair involves moving a reveal, changing how a scene unfolds or writing a new landing, choose `sequence`. Identify the connected editable passage in `sequence_ranges` with short exact quotes from its first and last utterances, inclusive. They may be in the same utterance. This gives the writer room to compose the join. Choose `whole_episode` when the underlying route across the episode needs rebuilding. Use `local` for repairs that leave the surrounding sequence in place. For a local deletion quote all the words to remove; two surviving phrases do not authorize deleting the words between them.

Now hear individual lines for idiom, grammar, cadence and the speaker's attitude. What does this aside do here? A dry joke needs no literal misconception to refute. Keep an earned laugh; remove an explanation that steps on it. A serious correction needs a real misunderstanding in the preceding words. Distinguish specialist knowledge from a restatement of an obvious consequence. Identify the exact terms of a genuine grammar error and require the correction even in a strong scene.

Use the worked edits below as illustrations of editing decisions. Quote only the submitted episode in the response. Give each required finding an exact quotation and zero-based utterance index, a concrete assignment and what to preserve. Use important or blocking for work required before recording, optional for equally recordable alternatives, and keep when no required change remains. Use duration estimates for pace. Leave `research_requests` empty; verification belongs to the evidence editor.

## Worked edits

**A listener needs one piece of shop knowledge.**
- Draft: “On the poster the cyclist's red cap floated an inch above her head. Inez laid the colour stencils over their pins. Two holes keep each stencil in place. If a hole gets wider, the stencil can move. Moving the stencil moves the colour. The left hole in the red stencil had torn into an oval.”
- Problem: The pins are new information. After that, the torn hole shows what went wrong.
- Assignment: Local. Keep how the stencils are held and what Inez finds. Cut the intervening lesson.
- Edited: “On the poster the cyclist's red cap floated an inch above her head. Inez laid the colour stencils over their pins. Two holes keep each stencil in place. The left hole in the red stencil had torn into an oval.”

**A dry line has done its job.**
- Draft: “Nora tried to open her new scissors. She pulled at the plastic. She tried her teeth. ‘I know a tool for this,’ she said. She meant the scissors inside the package.”
- Problem: We can see what Nora can see. The following explanation takes the laugh away from her.
- Assignment: Local. Keep the struggle and Nora's own line exactly; end there.
- Edited: “Nora tried to open her new scissors. She pulled at the plastic. She tried her teeth. ‘I know a tool for this,’ she said.”

**The answer arrives before anyone has a reason to want it.**
- Draft: “An old register identified the boy as Nico Vera. In Kit's classroom photograph every child had a name except the one with the drum. Kit had tried matching his face to other photographs. A builder later found the school band register behind a wall. It listed the musicians by instrument. There was a single entry for drum, Nico Vera. So the search was successful. Photographs and records work well together.”
- Problem: Giving the name away makes the search idle. Cutting only the final summary would still leave the reveal in the wrong place.
- Assignment: Sequence, from the first mention of the register through the close. Give Kit her unanswered question, keep the failed search by face, then let the recovered register change how we look. Compose the last beat from its actual entry.
- Edited: “In Kit's classroom photograph, only the boy with the drum had no name. She tried matching his face to other photographs. Then a builder found the school band register behind a wall. The musicians were listed by instrument. This time Kit didn't need to recognize his face. Under ‘Drum’ there was one name: Nico Vera.”
