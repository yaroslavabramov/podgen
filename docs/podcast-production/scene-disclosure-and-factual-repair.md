# Narrative disclosure and factual repair

A plan can promise a discovery and inadvertently give it away in an earlier transition. Read the opening, neighboring acts and the exact transition into the scene before judging whether a reveal works. A complete synopsis for the writer can disclose an outcome that the listener should discover later. The speaking order determines when that happens.

## Reviewing a plan

Identify what the listener already knows at the first intended reveal. If the preceding act has supplied the same explanation, determine what remains for the scene to accomplish. The plan editor can move a fact, change the question, or choose a different scene. The test is whether the sequence changes the audience’s understanding.

## Reviewing speech and evidence

The script reader follows the utterances in order. A comment after a revealing scene may give it emotional weight, explain a consequence or lead to a new question. If it only repeats the same conclusion, removing it can improve both pace and clarity. Read the new join before assigning the change.

A fact-checker examines the claim the audience actually hears, including units, populations, timing and attribution. A qualification in a source limits what can be asserted. It does not oblige the writer to discuss every alternative. If a number or qualifier is extraneous, ask whether removing the unsupported assertion preserves the useful meaning. The reviser checks downstream comparisons and calculations when changing a figure.

## Verification

Compile the complete model-facing inputs to check delivery of passages, role instructions and exact references. Then inspect an actual plan with its separate review and an actual script before and after revision. Evidence of correct request assembly cannot determine whether the story works for a listener.
