# Writing for the ear

A reader can stop at an unfamiliar word and reread the previous sentence. A listener usually keeps going. When a later discovery depends on an unfamiliar object, establish what it does in the present scene before asking the audience to reason about it.

## The brief

The brief contains the user’s question, intended audience, language, format, duration and any preferences the user actually expressed. The dossier holds possible explanations and evidence. The planner chooses the opening and route. A brief about GPS does not need to tell one host to introduce two imaginary lighthouses; the writer can invent a useful teaching example once the technical relationship is understood.

## From a dossier to a story

Try telling the episode in one paragraph. What does the listener see or wonder first? Which observation forces a different explanation? What answer can the audience now reach? A list of subjects with “and then” between them is a research outline, not yet a sequence of discoveries.

A mechanical explanation often benefits from following a signal or physical object. A historical investigation can follow what participants knew at the time of a decision. Alternative accounts deserve room when a concrete observation could distinguish them. Decide where the story earns its time. An unusual but irrelevant detail can remain in the dossier.

For an opening about GPS, begin with an everyday action and introduce the need for a clock when the receiver measures the time a signal took to arrive. Starting with “how can an inaccurate clock determine a position?” demands the listener already know why a clock belongs in the story. A familiar situation has value only if it leads into the real mechanism.

## Building a passage

Give each act a listener-facing purpose. The writer can open on a decision, shift scale, stage a comparison or tell a sourced scene. When an analogy does substantial work, explain what its parts represent and where its limits affect the conclusion. Express the useful limit briefly, in the sentence that needs it.

Dialogue works when the second speaker changes the course of the explanation: they notice a contradiction, make a prediction or follow a consequence. Alternating equivalent summaries produces the sound of two people reading one lecture. In a solo episode the same change can arise from a new document, a physical failure or a decision by a participant.

Reconstruct a scene when the reconstruction is clear and the details can carry their evidentiary weight. Historical witnesses, contemporary correspondence and retrospective recollections have different vantage points. A person can appear in a scene without being credited with later knowledge. Use attribution where it helps the listener place the observation. A standing list of cautions makes the writer narrate the editorial process.

## What to cut or keep

Read the whole paragraph before preserving a qualification. If “the brewery’s own well saved its workers” exceeds the evidence, state what the account establishes about the well and the water the workers used. Once that resolves the overstatement, an additional sentence disavowing every other explanation is usually redundant.

A detail earns its space by its effect in the final account. A woman who received water from Broad Street at a distance can explain why John Snow asked people what they drank. A full inventory of every category in a mortality table need only appear when the difference between those categories affects the listener's answer. The editor chooses what to commission; facts retained in the dossier are available for checking.

## Read the result aloud

Start with the words the listener hears. Mark the first point of confusion and say what information was missing there. Only then inspect the plan, draft and sources to diagnose it. A production note that reads “make the contrast clear” cannot rescue a passage where neither speaker makes the contrast audible.

At the ending, try cutting the last recap and listen to the join. Keep a callback that lands differently after the story. Remove a repeated instruction to understand the same point. Rebuild a whole passage when the original transition still moves the story backwards after its wording has changed. Judge the delivered speech rather than the writer's explanation of its revisions.
